## What “DMIT Los Angeles” actually gives you

DMIT describes Los Angeles as its flagship North American node, distributed across CoreSite and Digital Realty. The company says the location is positioned at a major Pacific interconnection point and has the highest network capacity in its lineup, with a stated 3.8 Tbps Tier 1 capacity figure for the LAX location.

The broader DMIT network is built around three Pacific locations—Los Angeles, Hong Kong, and Tokyo—and the company says its network connects directly or through optimized routes to China Telecom, China Unicom, and China Mobile International. For Los Angeles deployments, that matters because the main reason to choose DMIT is often the path to Asia rather than merely having a server physically located in California.

The three LAX network families are worth understanding before looking at individual plans:

**Premium** combines Tier 1 transit with premium routing partners, including China Telecom CN2 GIA. DMIT positions it for workloads where China and Asia-Pacific access is important, such as cross-border applications, e-commerce sites, media delivery, and latency-sensitive services.

**Eyeball** uses Tier 1 transit plus reasonable-effort China routing through CMIN2/CMI and other Chinese eyeball networks. DMIT explicitly says it does not carry the same routing guarantees as Premium, while positioning it as a more budget-conscious option for mixed China/global traffic.

**Tier 1** drops the China-specific optimization and concentrates on routing across Asia-Pacific, North America, and Europe. DMIT describes this family as suited to backup servers, CI/CD, monitoring, general compute, and workloads that do not specifically need China-optimized routing.

That distinction is more useful than simply asking whether one DMIT plan has more CPU than another. A 4-vCPU plan on a China-optimized network and a 4-vCPU Tier 1 plan are designed around different jobs.

## Which LAX network makes sense for which workload?

For a China-facing application, the first question should usually be **routing profile**, not “how many vCPUs can I get for the money?”

DMIT’s Premium network is the one explicitly built around China-optimized routing, including China Telecom CN2 GIA. The company lists corporate and e-commerce sites, media delivery, low-latency game servers, and cross-border applications among the relevant use cases.

That makes plans such as the LAX.AS3.Pro or the newer LAX.AN5.Pro family the logical part of the catalog to investigate when traffic into China is genuinely important.

The catch is that **AS3 is currently under active optimization**. DMIT’s own Los Angeles pricing page warns that the LAX AS3 series may have reduced disk performance and a lower SLA while the platform is being built out. That warning is current and should not be buried just because the entry price looks attractive.

The AN5 platform is DMIT’s newest listed hardware family. DMIT describes AN5 as AMD EPYC 9005 series, with Zen 5, DDR5 memory, and PCIe 5.0 NVMe storage at the platform level. AN4 uses AMD EPYC 9004 series and Zen 4, while AS3 uses AMD EPYC 7003 series and Zen 3.

For a mixed China/global audience, **Eyeball** is the middle ground. DMIT says it uses reasonable-effort China routing via CMIN2/CMI and other Chinese eyeball ISPs, but it does not carry the same routing guarantees as Premium. The AN5 EB range also has much larger published transfer quotas than the corresponding Premium plans—for example, AN5 EB MINI is listed at 10 TB versus 5 TB for AN5 Pro MINI.

For workloads where China-specific routing is not the point, **Tier 1** is easier to justify. The LAX AN5 T1 VOLUME range starts at $14.90/month with 5 TB of transfer and scales all the way to 160 TB on the $199.90/month V12C24G configuration. The GENERAL range starts at $16.90/month.

That makes the T1 side particularly relevant for backup servers, CI/CD workers, monitoring nodes, relay infrastructure, and general-purpose US/Asia workloads where premium China routing is unnecessary. DMIT itself lists those use cases for Tier 1.

## Current pricing is not the whole cost calculation

DMIT includes free instant setup for its Cloud Instance products and provides full root access. Its Cloud Instance documentation also says deployment can be completed in minutes, with one-click system installation and SSH-based access.

The bandwidth model needs more attention than the headline port speed. DMIT documents multiple transfer-overload behaviors, including suspension, throttling, and no-restriction models. When a package exceeds its quota, the available response can depend on the particular product. The documentation says customers may be able to pay for a quota reset or wait for the next reset date.

For the LAX T1 products, the current pricing page explicitly shows transfer as **“Max (IN, OUT)”**, which corresponds to a maximum-of-the-two-directions style quota rather than simply adding every inbound and outbound byte together.

The refund policy is another point worth reading before ordering. DMIT’s current Terms of Service, updated January 22, 2026, state that a full refund may be available for a new service purchased no more than three days earlier when VM transfer usage does not exceed 30 GB, with a partial-refund framework available for new orders within 30 days under the specified conditions. The same terms say successfully processed renewal invoices are non-refundable. So treating the first purchase and the automatic renewal as though they have the same cancellation rules would be a mistake.

One practical rule is to test the **actual route you care about** before committing to a long billing term. A Los Angeles-to-Chicago path and a Los Angeles-to-mainland-China path are different networking problems.

Also check the exact quota model on the order page. DMIT documents both bidirectional and unidirectional-maximum accounting, and over-quota handling can vary by package.
